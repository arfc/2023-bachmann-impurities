\section{Methodology}
The X-energy Xe-100 and 
the \gls*{USNC} \gls*{MMR} were selected for analysis in this work. These two reactors provide comparisons across 
some notable reactor design spaces, such as power output
and fuel residence time. Each of these design factors are expected to 
be impacted by a changing fuel composition. 
Additionally, there is enough publicly available information about 
each design to model them with a reasonable degree of accuracy. 
We created models of each core and performed neutronics analysis using 
Serpent 2\cite{leppanen_serpent_2015} with the 
ENDF/B VII.1 cross section library \cite{chadwick_endfb-vii1_2011}.
The core geometries used are not 
the exact geometries designed by X-energy and \gls*{USNC} for the 
Xe-100 and \gls*{MMR}, respectively, but 
are representative designs based on publicly-available information 
\cite{mulder_overview_2021,noauthor_usnc_2021,hawari_development_2018,venneri_micro_2019}. 
The serpentTools python package \cite{johnson_serpenttools_2020} 
was used to 
analyze the results from the models.

\subsection{Sangamon200 model} \label{sec:xe100_serpent_model}
The Xe-100 is a 200 MWth, 80 MWe, \acrfull*{TRISO}-fueled pebble-bed 
\gls*{HTGR} \cite{mulder_overview_2021}. Some important design parameters 
of the Xe-100 are listed in Table \ref{tab:xe-100}. 
The Sangmon200 model from \cite{richter_isotopic_2022}, shown in 
Figure \ref{fig:xe100_core}, served as a base model 
to represent the Xe-100. The model itself can be 
found in the Phlox repository on GitHub, with this work using 
the archived version on Zenodo \cite{richter_zoerichterphlox_2022}.
The Sangamon200 model is a \gls*{TRISO}-fueled, pebble-bed, \gls*{HTGR}-style 
reactor core model initially created to characterize the isotopic 
compositions and reactor physics of a 200 MWth pebble-bed \gls*{HTGR}.

While this reactor model 
is very similar to current published data for the X-energy Xe-100
\cite{mulder_overview_2021}, two notable differences affect 
the reactor physics. The first difference is that the \gls*{TRISO} particles 
in the Sangamon200 are modeled as homogeneous materials instead of 
the explicit layers, with a description of the homogenization in 
\cite{richter_isotopic_2022}. Using a homogenized \gls*{TRISO} particle 
decreases the \keff of the core by 4.45\%, but it does not reduce the 
thermal and fast neutron fluxes across the reactor outside of error 
\cite{richter_isotopic_2022}. Additionally, homogenizing the pebbles 
is expected to lower the strength of the Doppler broadening effects, 
which will lower the magnitude (i.e., make less negative) the reactivity feedback
coefficients. Finally, the homogenization will increase the flux 
in the core, because self-shielding in the pebbles is negligible. 
The magnitude of effects from homogenizing the pebbles may 
vary some with changing fuel compositions, but the effects 
are expected to remain relatively constant across the compositions 
in this work.  Given that the heavy metal content of the pebbles remains constant
across the three compositions in this work, the reactor physics effects 
from changing the compositions are expected to be larger in magnitude 
than the effects from pebble homogenization.
Therefore, it is reasonable to assume homogenized pebbles in order to 
balance the goals of this work with available computational resources.

The second 
notable difference is the reactor vessel shape. The Sangamon200 is a simple 
cylinder, while the Xe-100 is a cylinder with a cone at the bottom to funnel 
the pebbles to a single point as they come out of the core. However, 
this is a less neutronically important region of the core, and the 
geometry remained the same as that of the Sangamon200 for 
consistency with those published results. 

In the Xe-100 reactor, each pebble moves downward through the
core while it burns. Each 
time that a pebble travels from the top of the core to the bottom is a single 
pass. Once a pebble reaches the bottom of the core, it is removed, 
inspected to determine its burnup level, and placed back in 
the core for another pass if the pebble has 
not reached the target burnup. Each pebble is expected to 
go through multiple passes in the core, an average 
of six passes per pebble, before reaching the target burnup of 168 
MWd/kgHM. To approximate the pebble burnup variation in an 
equilibrium core, the model contains
six different pebble isotopic compositions to  
represent a pebble on each of its six sequential passes through 
the core. 
Applying the methodology 
Richter developed for the Sangamon200 
\cite{richter_isotopic_2022} (depletion of a single pebble model 
with heterogeneous \gls*{TRISO} layers) yielded the isotopic 
composition of the pebbles after each pass.
1/6th of the pebbles of the core contain each composition. 
The pebbles with different burnups 
were mixed throughout the core, mimicking the spread of 
differently burned pebbles throughout a core in the equilibrium state 
of the reactor. Each pebble at the same pass number has the same 
composition in this model.   

The Sangamon200 model in this work is in an isothermal state 
at 800 K and run 
with 100,000 particles per cycle, 50 inactive cycles, 
and 200 active cycles at the equilibrium state previously described. 
Our model does not capture pebble movement or the continuous 
refueling scheme in a pebble-bed \gls*{HTGR}. 

\subsection{MMR-like model}
The \gls*{USNC}\footnote{Since the initial writing of 
this article, this reactor design has been acquired by NANO Nuclear, 
\cite{noauthor_nano_2025}} \gls*{MMR} is a 15 MWth, 5 MWe, prismatic \gls*{HTGR} that 
uses \gls*{TRISO} fuel in \gls*{FCM} pellets inside graphite blocks 
\cite{noauthor_usnc_2021}. Table \ref{tab:mmr} lists some of the important 
design parameters of this reactor.
We created an \gls*{MMR}-like model primarily based on information in 
a paper by Hawari and Venneri
\cite{hawari_development_2018}, and supplemented or modified based on 
information published by \gls*{USNC} \cite{noauthor_usnc_2021,venneri_micro_2019}. 
Information about the \gls*{TRISO} 
particle and \gls*{FCM} pellets was found in \cite{noauthor_usnc_2021}
and information about the graphite block dimensions and configuration 
was found via visual inspection of figures in \cite{venneri_micro_2019}. 
The final core configuration 
is different than what Hawari and Venneri modeled \cite{hawari_development_2018} 
because their model is designed to operate 
for 10 years, while the \gls*{MMR} is designed to operate for 20 years 
\cite{noauthor_usnc_2021}. The model developed achieves a burnup 
of 81.04 MWd/kgHM, compared to the 82.6 MWd/kgHM reported by 
\gls*{USNC} \cite{noauthor_usnc_2021}.

Figure \ref{fig:mmr_core} shows radial and axial slices of the 
\gls*{MMR} model. The fuel channels have a radius of 1.15 cm, 
estimated based on the same
size as the \gls*{FCM} pellets. 
The coolant channels have a radius of 
3 cm, arbitrarily chosen because no specific information was found.
The control rod channels have a radius of 6 cm, obtained from 
\cite{hawari_development_2018}. These dimensions affect the graphite volume 
of the core, and thus affect the fuel to moderator ratio of the core. 
The \gls*{TRISO} particles are modeled with a 40\% packing fraction in 
the \gls*{FCM} particles \cite{powers_fully_2014}.
Control rods and burnable poisons are not included in this model, or 
the Sangamon200 model, so the control rod locations are filled with helium.
There are five layers of the graphite fuel blocks 
stacked to form the entire core, to approximate the number of 
fuel blocks described in \cite{noauthor_usnc_2021}. The entire core 
is assumed to be in an isothermal state at 800 K. There is a 20 cm 
thick graphite reflector above and below the stacks of graphite, 
and a 10 cm thick beryllium-oxide reflector on the outside of the 
graphite blocks of the core, illustrated by the green material in 
Figure \ref{fig:mmr_core}. While Hawari and Venneri \cite{hawari_development_2018}
define both of these reflectors in their model, they only 
define the thickness of the beryllium-oxide reflector. Therefore we 
assumed a graphite reflector thickness of 20 cm to provide 3-5 mean 
free paths of material. These model parameters are summarized in 
Table \ref{tab:mmr_design}. The input files for this model can be found 
in a Zenodo archive 
\cite{bachmann_mmr-like_2023}.

\subsection{Temperature variations and feedbacks}
The fuel impurities introduced by downblending may have an effect on 
fission products and neutron precursors present, which will have 
a direct effect on reactivity. 
The metrics in this work include the reactivity temperature feedback 
coefficients ($\alpha$). To investigate this metric, the models include 
variations in the fuel, coolant, and moderator temperatures 
between 700, 750, 800, 850, and 900 K to calculate the fuel 
($\alpha_F$), coolant ($\alpha_C$), moderator ($\alpha_M$), 
and total ($\alpha_T$) temperature feedback coefficients, assuming a linear 
relationship. Variations in the material densities accompanied the 
temperature variations in the cross section libraries 
(including S($\alpha$, $\beta$) for the graphite) of 
the models. Empirical relationships 
between density and temperature (Eq. \ref{eq:uranium_density} 
\cite{fink_thermophysical_2000}) provided the density 
changes in the UO$_2$ of the \gls*{MMR}-like model,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uranium_density}
    \rho(T) = \rho(273)\times\left(\frac{L_{273}}{L_T}\right)^3,
\end{equation}
in which $L_{273}$ and $L_T$ are the lengths of the UO$_2$ at 
273 K and the temperature in question (in Kelvin), calculated 
using Eq. \ref{eq:uranium_length} \cite{fink_thermophysical_2000}.
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uranium_length}
    L_T = L_{273}(9.973\times10^{-1} + 9.082\times10^{-6}T
    -2.705\times10^{-10}T^2 + 4.391\times10^{-13}T^3)
\end{equation}
The changes in 
the UCO density in the Sangamon200 model were also based on 
these relationships, assuming they still hold true for the 
different material. 
Graphite density changes were linearly extrapolated from the 
data available in \cite{mceligot_thermal_2016}. 
Data from \cite{petersen_properties_1970} (Table 
\ref{tab:helium_density}) was used to account for the density 
variations in the helium coolant as a function of temperature. 
The \gls*{MMR}-like model assumes an inlet pressure of 3 MPa 
\cite{noauthor_usnc_2021}, which required linear interpolations 
between the 2 and 4 MPa data. The Sangamon200 model assumes 
and inlet pressure of 6 MPa \cite{mulder_overview_2021}.

\subsection{Fuel compositions}
This paper uses three different \gls*{HALEU} compositions: pure 
\gls*{HALEU}, \gls*{HALEU} derived from the spent \gls*{EBR} fuel inventory, 
and \gls*{HALEU} derived from the Y-12 \gls*{HEU} 
inventory. The pure fuel composition assumes that all uranium present is 
either $^{235}$U or $^{238}$U, and at the desired enrichment level for each 
reactor (ignoring an $^{234}$U that would normally be present in 
enriched natural uranium). The \gls*{EBR} 
composition is based on the average uranium isotopic composition from 
downblending spent fuel from \gls*{EBR}, published by \gls*{INL} 
\cite{vaden_isotopic_2018}. The Y-12 composition is based on the 
estimated uranium isotopic composition from downblending \gls*{HEU} 
stockpiles at the Y-12 National Security Complex \cite{nelson_foreign_2010}.
The published compositions for the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 fuel 
assume an enrichment 
of 19.75\%, and the \gls*{MMR} requires this enrichment level. 
Therefore, the uranium isotopic ratios 
published are directly applied for the \gls*{MMR} fuel. For the fuel in the 
Xe-100, the isotopic fractions had to be adjusted slightly to match the 
needed enrichment level of 15.5\%. The $^{235}$U fraction was set to match 
the 
enrichment level required, the non-$^{238}$U isotopes were kept in the 
same weight fraction defined in the publications, and the $^{238}$U was 
defined to fill the remainder of the fuel. Therefore, all 
three fuel compositions for each 
reactor have the same $^{235}$U weight fractions, have non-$^{238}$U 
weight
fractions that match the published values, and have varying $^{238}$U 
weight fractions for each reactor design. Table \ref{tab:u_comps} 
provides the composition used for each reactor model and each fuel source 
in this work. This table reports the uranium atom fractions 
without the oxygen 
and/or carbon fractions defined. Therefore, the totals in each 
column for a reactor model do not sum to one. Each of the two 
downblended \gls*{HEU} compositions meet the ASTM C1462-21 
standard for 
uranium enriched to less than 20\% \cite{noauthor_standard_2021}.
