\section{Results}
Results from each model include the effective neutron 
multiplication factor (\keff), effective delayed 
neutron fraction (\betaEff), energy- and spatially dependent 
neutron flux ($\phi$), as well as the fuel, coolant, moderator, and total reactivity 
temperature feedback coefficients ($\alpha_F$, $\alpha_C$, 
$\alpha_M$, $\alpha_T$). The
\keff, \betaEff, and neutron flux were calculated by Serpent. 
Neutron energy binning is based on the pre-defined SCALE-238 
group energy structure in 
Serpent for the energy-dependent flux and the pre-defined 
two group energy structure to separate the 
spatially-dependent flux into fast and thermal energy groups. The delineation between fast 
and thermal neutrons with this group structure is 0.625 eV.
A linear function fit to the
data (reactivity vs. material temperature) yielded the 
reactivity feedback coefficients, $\alpha$, with error 
propagated.

For the Sangamon200 reactor, these metrics are only compared for each fuel composition in an 
equilibrium state with pebbles at burnup levels to represent 
different numbers of passes. For the \gls*{MMR}-like reactor these metrics 
are compared at \gls*{BOL} (0 MWd/kgHM burnup), \gls*{MOL} (40.52 MWd/kgHM burnup), 
and \gls*{EOL} (81.04 MWd/kgHM burnup). Each metric provides  
insight into the performance of 
the reactor, such as the materials degradation rate, amount of burnable 
poisons required, control rod worth, and the cycle time.

three different \gls*{HALEU} compositions in the Sangamon200 model. All three fuel compositions 
result in a slightly super-critical \keff.
The \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 fuels result in a \keff 
1482 $\pm$ 47 pcm and 1417 $\pm$ 47 pcm, respectively, 
greater than the \keff from the pure fuel.

The uranium isotopes mostly present in 
each of the fuel compositions (atom fraction of at least 1$\times 10^{-3}$)
are $^{234}$U, $^{235}$U, $^{236}$U, and $^{238}$U (see 
Table \ref{tab:u_comps}). The $^{235}$U weight 
fraction is the same in each fuel composition, so the impurities are 
displacing the $^{238}$U in the fuel. $^{234}$U and $^{236}$U have larger 
thermal 
total fission cross sections than $^{238}$U, so their displacement of the
$^{238}$U in the fuel increases the neutron multiplication of the reactor. 
This is supported by the impure fuels leading to an increase of 
0.0248-0.0250
in the thermal fission factor, $\eta$ (Table \ref{tab:xe100_eta}), which 
signifies a greater ratio of the number of neutrons
born from fission to the number absorbed in the fuel for the impure 
fuels. The change in $\eta$ is relevant because it is fuel specific, 
but alone it does not define \keff.
Part of the increase in \keff when using the impure fuels is because 
the pebbles modeled are at different burnup steps. Many of the 
pebbles are already partially burned and the uranium impurities in the fuel 
have already undergone neutron capture reactions and have been transmuted 
into other isotopes that are more fissile. For example, 
the partially burned, impure fuel pebbles may have more 
$^{235}$U than the partially burned, pure fuel pebbles 
because of the transmutation of $^{234}$U into $^{235}$U. 
The effect of an increased \keff from the impure fuels is that more 
control mechanisms may be required to control the chain reaction, or 
to lower the \keff and ensure that the targeted discharge burnup and 
cycle length can be reached.

\subsubsection{Effective delayed neutron fraction comparison}
The effective delayed neutron fraction, \betaEff, resulting 
from the use of each fuel type is reported in 
Table \ref{tab:betaeff_xe100}. The \betaEff when using the 
\gls*{EBR} and Y-12 fuels is 0.00011 $\pm$ 0.00008 and 
0.00016 $\pm$ 0.0008, respectively, smaller than when using 
the pure fuel. The pure fuel results in 
a larger \betaEff than the impure fuels because the other 
uranium isotopes present in the impure fuels breed into 
fissile material that has a smaller \betaEff than $^{235}$U. 
The different compositions for each pass captures 
this breeding of fissile material with lower \betaEff values. 
The additional fissile material is not 
present in the pure fuel because it is the material that is bred in from 
the uranium impurities. The smaller \betaEff indicates 
that control mechanisms in the reactor will be less effective 
because a larger fraction of the neutron population will be prompt neutrons, and there is a smaller time scale on which 
the neutron population can be impacted. However, the decrease 
in \betaEff is very small and unlikely to prevent operator 
actions and control mechanisms from being effective.

\subsubsection{Neutron flux comparison} \label{sec:xe100_flux}
Figure \ref{fig:xe100_mg_flux} shows the neutron flux integrated 
across the volume of the core as a function of energy (top) and 
the difference from the pure fuel flux (bottom). The purple 
line in both plots shows the delineation between the fast 
and thermal energy groups in this work (0.625 eV). 
There is a large peak in the thermal flux around 0.01 eV. The impure 
\gls*{HALEU} compositions have a smaller thermal flux peak 
than the pure fuel at this energy, differing by 8.26 $\times 10^{11}$ 
$\frac{n}{cm^2s}$. At high energies (above 10
MeV) the impure fuels result in a flux up to 1.7 $\times 10^{7}$ 
$\frac{n}{cm^2s}$ larger flux than the pure fuel.
The smaller thermal flux with the impure fuels is in contrast to the larger 
\keff from the impure fuels. This difference in trend of the \keff and 
the neutron flux suggests that the impurities in the fuel lead to 
more neutrons born from fissions but the neutrons travel a shorter 
distance in the core before being absorbed.

Figures \ref{fig:xe100_thermal_radial}-\ref{fig:xe100_fast_axial} show 
thermal and fast fluxes in the reactor core axially and radially. 
One detector was defined for these spatial fluxes, with uniform binning
in the x-, y-, and z-axes.  Initially, Serpent 
integrates the results over the bin volume, and so the flux
presented here has been normalized by that same volume to 
remove the bin integration. 
Therefore, the plotted flux is in units of $\frac{n}{cm^2s}$ across a slice of the x- or z-axis and 
these figures do not capture any spatial integration. 

The thermal radial flux spectra from all three \gls*{HALEU} compositions 
(Figure \ref{fig:xe100_thermal_radial})
show peaks at the edge of the core resulting from the graphite moderator 
around the core. The flux from the impure fuels is similar to the flux from 
the pure fuel, differing between -3.89 $\times 10^{13}$ to 2.47 
$\times 10^{13}$ $\frac{n}{cm^2s}$ and -2.66$\times 10^{13}$ 
to 1.40$\times 10^{13}$ 
$\frac{n}{cm^2s}$ for the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 fuels, respectively. The error of the pure fuel flux ranges 
from 0 to 1.05 $\times 10^{13}$ $\frac{n}{cm^2s}$.

$^{238}$U has the largest difference between the total and 
fission cross sections of the uranium isotopes considered for this model. 
By replacing the $^{238}$U with other isotopes the flux should 
increase from the larger fission to total event ratio because the other isotopes 
are more likely to have fission reactions and produce neutrons. If some 
of the fissions are occurring from reactions in $^{233}$U (present 
in the \gls*{EBR} fuel, see Table \ref{tab:u_comps}) compared with 
$^{235}$U, then the distribution of fission products present in the partially 
burned pebbles will be different which affects the neutron reaction 
rate densities in the core. Further, the even-numbered uranium isotopes 
(e.g., $^{234}$U) have relatively small fission cross sections; 
they are more likely to absorb a neutron in a capture reaction than to fission. 

To highlight the effects of the partially-burned pebbles 
on the neutron flux, we shuffled the pebble placements 
in the core. The fresh pebbles were switched with the most 
burnt, etc. Figure \ref{fig:xe100_pebble_shuffle}
shows the thermal radial flux with the Y-12 fuel composition 
in the initial pebble placements and in the shuffled 
configuration. The two fluxes differ by up to 2.40 
$\times 10^{13}$ $\frac{n}{cm^2s}$, highlighting the 
of the effect of the random pebble packing 
arrangement on the neutron flux.
The difference from shuffling the pebbles is smaller 
than the differences caused by the different fuel 
compositions. By using the same pebble configuration 
with each fuel composition, the changes in the neutron 
flux can be attributed to the composition change, as 
opposed to pebble configuration changes.

The thermal axial flux in the pure fuel in this work peaks at about 
0.87 $\times 10^{14}$ $\frac{n}{cm^2s}$ and the fast axial flux for 
the pure fuel in this work peaks at about 1.90 $\times 10^{14}$
$\frac{n}{cm^2s}$. These results show an opposite pattern to the fluxes 
reported from modeling the Xe-100 by Mulder and Boyes \cite{mulder_neutronics_2020}, 
in which the thermal axial flux peaks around 1.3 $\times 10^{14}$
$\frac{n}{cm^2s}$ and the ast axial flux peaks around 0.7 $\times 10^{14}$
$\frac{n}{cm^2s}$. Part of the disagreement in the results comes from the 
different energy binning used in each work. Mulder and Boyes used $<$ 1.86 eV to 
define the thermal range and $>$ 0.1 MeV to define the fast range, while 
this work applies a boundary of 0.625 eV between the thermal and fast energy groups. 
The energy bins defined by Mulder and Boyes includes more energies in the thermal 
range and fewer energies in the fast range than this work, which can explain 
why the thermal flux they report is larger than the one in this work and the 
fast flux they report is smaller than the one in this work.

All of the coefficients are negative for each of the three fuel compositions.

The fuel reactivity feedback coefficient 
($\alpha_F$) from the pure fuel is 0.050 $\pm$ 0.161 pcm/K more negative than the $\alpha_F$ 
from the \gls*{EBR} fuel, but 0.165 $\pm$ 0.186 pcm/K less negative than the 
$\alpha_F$ from the Y-12 fuel. 
The coolant reactivity feedback coefficient ($\alpha_C$) from the pure fuel 
is 0.022 $\pm$ 0.307 pcm/K less negative than the $\alpha_C$ from the 
\gls*{EBR} fuel, and 0.181 $\pm$ 0.285 pcm/K more negative than the 
$\alpha_C$ from the Y-12 fuel. 
The moderator reactivity feedback coefficient ($\alpha_M$) from the pure fuel 
is 0.409 $\pm$ 0.503 pcm/K less negative than the $\alpha_M$ from the 
\gls*{EBR} fuel, and 0.490 $\pm$ 0.404 pcm/K less negative than the 
$\alpha_M$ from the Y-12 fuel. 
The total reactivity feedback coefficient ($\alpha_T$) from the pure fuel 
is 0.618 $\pm$ 0.605 pcm/K less negative than the $\alpha_T$ from the 
\gls*{EBR} fuel, and 1.050 $\pm$ 0.612 pcm/K less negative than the 
$\alpha_T$ from the Y-12 fuel. 

The changes in the reactivity feedback coefficients in the impure fuels 
are either within error of the coefficient with the pure fuels, are 
more negative than the coefficient with the pure fuel, or both. Based on these 
results, one can conclude that these fuel compositions will not prevent 
large changes in these reactor performance metrics, or prevent the reactor 
from operating in an unsafe condition (e.g., a positive $\alpha_T$ value). 

The feedback coefficients are within the ranges reported 
by Mulder and Boyes \cite{mulder_neutronics_2020} for their 
Xe-100 model, despite the differences 
in the reactor models used.
The consistency between the feedback coefficient values from the impure 
fuels in this work and the values reported by Mulder and Boyes 
\cite{mulder_neutronics_2020} suggests that 
the impurities in the fuel do not greatly impact the temperature reactivity 
coefficients.

\subsection{MMR-like reactor}
Sections \ref{sec:mmr_keff}-\ref{sec:mmr_coeff} report and analyze 
the results of the 
\gls*{EBR} and Y-12 impurities for the \gls*{MMR}-like reactor model. 
The four different results are presented for three different 
burnup steps during the reactor operation: \gls*{BOL} (0 MWd/kgU),
\gls*{MOL} (40.52 MWd/kgU), and \gls*{EOL} (81.04 MWd/kgU). 

\subsubsection{Effective neutron multiplication factor comparison}\label{sec:mmr_keff}
Table \ref{tab:mmr_keff} reports the effective 
neutron multiplication factor, \keff, of the \gls*{MMR}-like 
model at the 
different burnup steps using each fuel composition. At \gls*{BOL} the 
\keff with the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 fuels are 1193 $\pm$ 40 pcm and 
1293 $\pm$ 40 pcm, respectively, smaller than the \keff from the pure fuel. 
At \gls*{MOL} the 
\keff with the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 fuels are 934 $\pm$ 36 pcm and 
1019 $\pm$ 35 pcm, respectively, smaller than the \keff from the pure fuel. At 
\gls*{EOL} the 
\keff with the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 fuels are 763 $\pm$ 37 pcm and 
911 $\pm$ 35 pcm, respectively, smaller than the \keff from the pure fuel. Each 
of these differences are significant (i.e., larger than the error), and the 
impure fuels results in smaller \keff values at each 
burnup step. 

The difference in \keff 
from the different fuel compositions decreases with burnup because of 
the depletion of the uranium, which includes burning of the parasitic 
uranium isotopes, $^{234}$U and $^{236}$U. 
All of the fuel in this model is unburned in the 
first burnup step, meaning that the impurities have a more significant 
effect on the neutron population at the beginning of life. 
The greater effect of the impurities at the \gls*{BOL} burnup 
step leads to the decreasing difference in \keff from 
the impure fuels.

\subsubsection{Effective delayed neutron fraction comparison}
Table \ref{tab:mmr_betaeff} reports the effective delayed 
neutron fraction, \betaEff, values when using 
each fuel composition at the different burnup steps. At \gls*{BOL} the 
\betaEff values from the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 fuel compositions differ from 
the \betaEff from the pure fuel by -0.00001 $\pm$ 0.00005 and 0.00007 
$\pm$ 0.00005, respectively. At \gls*{MOL} the 
\betaEff values from the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 fuel compositions differ from 
the \betaEff from the pure fuel by 0.00012 $\pm$ 0.00005 and 0.00005 
$\pm$ 0.00005, respectively. At \gls*{EOL} the 
\betaEff values from the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 fuel compositions differ from 
the \betaEff from the pure fuel by 0.00016 $\pm$ 0.00005 and 0.00012 
$\pm$ 0.00005, respectively. 

Some of the differences in the \betaEff values when using the impure fuels are 
larger than error. However, if the difference is larger than the error, the \betaEff
with the impure fuels is larger than the \betaEff with the pure fuel at that burnup step. 
Therefore, a larger fraction of the neutrons are delayed neutrons, which means that 
the neutron control mechanisms can be more effective with the impure fuels than with 
the pure fuels.

For all three 
fuel compositions, \betaEff decreases with increasing burnup. This trend
is consistent with the depletion of the $^{235}$U in the core and 
an increase in the number of fissions happening in isotopes
that have a lower \betaEff than $^{235}$U, such as $^{239}$Pu. 
The \betaEff values at \gls*{BOL} are all slightly larger

or \betaEff of a typical \gls*{PWR}. The consistency of the 
\betaEff being
larger than 0.0064 at \gls*{BOL} 
is a result of the smaller geometry of this core compared with a 
\gls*{PWR} core.
The small size of the core and higher enrichment cause an 
decrease in the probability of non-leakage of fast neutrons to 
dominate the decrease of the fast fission factor, compared with a \gls*{PWR}. 

\subsubsection{Neutron flux comparison}
Figure \ref{fig:mmr_energy_spectrum} shows the flux integrated across the 
core at \gls*{BOL} as a function of energy (top) and the difference between 
the flux from the pure fuel and each of the impure fuels (bottom). The differences 
in the bottom plot of Figure \ref{fig:mmr_energy_spectrum} range from 
-0.270 $\times 10^{10}$ to 0.868 $\times 10^{10}$ $\frac{n}{cm^2s}$ for the 
\gls*{EBR} fuel and they range from -0.282 $\times 10^{17}$ to 0.967 
$\times 10^{10}$ $\frac{n}{cm^2s}$ for the Y-12 fuel. These differences are 
two orders of magnitude smaller than the fluxes, but the error in the pure 
fuel flux is 0 to 0.144 $\times 10^{10}$ $\frac{n}{cm^2s}$. 

Generally, the impure fuels result in 
larger fluxes, noted by the peak in the thermal range and the difference in the fast energy 
range in the bottom plot of Figure \ref{fig:mmr_energy_spectrum}. Combined 
with the smaller \keff values when using the impure fuels (see Table 
\ref{tab:mmr_keff}) and the similar or larger \betaEff values with the impure fuels 
(see Table \ref{tab:mmr_betaeff}), there are more neutrons in the core when using 
the impure fuels, but they are not necessarily born from fission.

At \gls*{BOL}, the $\alpha_F$ values from the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 compositions 
differ from the pure fuel $\alpha_F$ by 0.222 $\pm$ 0.152 pcm/K and 
0.104 $\pm$ 0.142 pcm/K, respectively. At \gls*{MOL}, the $\alpha_F$ 
values from the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 compositions 
differ from the pure fuel $\alpha_F$ by 0.193 $\pm$ 0.097 pcm/K and 
0.218 $\pm$ 0.190 pcm/K, respectively. The impure fuels 
results in less negative $\alpha_F$ values than the pure fuel
at \gls*{BOL} and \gls*{MOL} that are outside the range of 
error. The displacement of $^{238}$U by the other isotopes 
in the impure fuels decreases the Doppler broadening effects 
of the fuel, given that $^{238}$U has more resonances than 
the other uranium isotopes. This effect on the $\alpha_F$ 
for these burnup steps must be accounted for to ensure 
safe operation. 
At \gls*{EOL}, the $\alpha_F$ 
values from the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 compositions 
differ from the pure fuel $\alpha_F$ by -0.222 $\pm$ 0.337 
pcm/K and -0.214 $\pm$ 0.426 pcm/K, respectively. The 
impure fuels lead to more negative $\alpha_F$ values than 
the pure fuel, but the differences are within error. 

At \gls*{BOL}, the $\alpha_C$ values from the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 compositions 
differ from the pure fuel $\alpha_C$ by -0.053 $\pm$ 0.181 pcm/K and 
-0.129 $\pm$ 0.279 pcm/K, respectively. At \gls*{MOL}, the $\alpha_C$ 
values from the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 compositions 
differ from the pure fuel $\alpha_C$ by -0.229 $\pm$ 0.156 pcm/K and 
-0.368 $\pm$ 0.187 pcm/K, respectively. At \gls*{EOL}, the $\alpha_C$ 
values from the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 compositions 
differ from the pure fuel $\alpha_C$ by -0.260 $\pm$ 0.300 pcm/K and 
-0.115 $\pm$ 0.386 pcm/K, respectively. At each of the burnup 
steps the impure fuels lead to more negative $\alpha_C$ values 
than the pure fuel, although some of the differences are 
within error. These results suggest that the impure fuels 
will lead to no significant change on this reactor 
performance metric or will assist in meeting safety 
requirements. 

At \gls*{BOL}, the $\alpha_M$ values from the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 compositions 
differ from the pure fuel $\alpha_M$ by 0.094 $\pm$ 0.234 pcm/K and 
-0.030 $\pm$ 0.245 pcm/K, respectively. At \gls*{MOL}, the $\alpha_M$ 
values from the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 compositions 
differ from the pure fuel $\alpha_M$ by 0.084 $\pm$ 0.211 pcm/K and 
-0.092 $\pm$ 0.277 pcm/K, respectively. For these two burnup 
steps the \gls*{EBR} fuel leads to a less negative $\alpha_M$ value than the pure fuel, and the Y-12 composition leads 
to more negative $\alpha_M$ values. However, the differences are within 
error. At \gls*{EOL}, the $\alpha_M$ 
values from the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 compositions 
differ from the pure fuel $\alpha_M$ by -0.265 $\pm$ 0.291 pcm/K and 
-0.183 $\pm$ 0.434 pcm/K, respectively. Both impure fuel 
compositions lead to more negative $\alpha_M$ values than the 
pure fuel, but the differences are still within error. 
Because all of the differences are within error, these fuel 
compositions do not lead to significant changes in this 
reactor performance metric. 


At \gls*{BOL}, the $\alpha_T$ values from the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 compositions 
differ from the pure fuel $\alpha_T$ by -0.098 $\pm$ 0.291 pcm/K and 
-0.116 $\pm$ 0.284 pcm/K, respectively. At \gls*{MOL}, the $\alpha_T$ 
values from the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 compositions 
differ from the pure fuel $\alpha_T$ by -0.137 $\pm$ 0.382 pcm/K and 
-0.171 $\pm$ 0.375 pcm/K, respectively. At \gls*{EOL}, the $\alpha_T$ 
values from the \gls*{EBR} and Y-12 compositions 
differ from the pure fuel $\alpha_T$ by 0.048 $\pm$ 0.508 pcm/K and 
0.202 $\pm$ 0.623 pcm/K, respectively. Opposite to the 
$\alpha_F$ values, the impure fuels lead to more 
negative values at the \gls*{BOL} and \gls*{MOL} burnup steps 
and less negative values at the \gls*{EOL} burnup step.
This change in pattern indicates a weak contribution of 
the fuel to $\alpha_T$ value.  
All of the differences are within error, 
suggesting that these fuel compositions do not lead to 
significant changes in the $\alpha_T$ value for this reactor
design.
