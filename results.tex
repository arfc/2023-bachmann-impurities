\section{Results}

The output files from Serpent were parsed and analyzed using the 
``serpentTools'' python package \cite{johnson_serpenttools_2020}. The 
\keff, neutron flux, and \betaEff were output by Serpent. The reactivity 
feedback coefficients were calculated by fitting a linear function to the 
data (material temperature vs reactivity) and propagating error through 
the fit. The neutron flux was divided into two energy groups for 
fast and thermal fluxes. We applied the default two-group structure in Serpent, 
in which the cutoff between the energy groups is 0.625 eV.

\subsection{MMR-like reactor}
The results of the MMR-like reactor are presented below. The \keff 
values are presented first, followed by the neutron fluxes, the 
\betaEff, and the reactivity coefficients. The burnup steps for 
the results, \gls{BOL}, mid-cycle, and \gls{EOL}, correspond 
to burnup values of 0, 40.52, and 81.04 MWd/kgU. The discharge 
burnup is within 2\% of the stated average discharge burnup for this 
reactor design \cite{noauthor_usnc_2021}.

\subsubsection{k-eff}
The fuel composition has an effect on the \keff value of the 
\gls{MMR}-like model, as shown by the values in Table \ref{tab:mmr_keff}.
The \keff value using all three fuel compositions decreases as a function 
of burnup, as expected because of the depletion of fissile material in the 
fuel. Using the \gls{EBR} or Y-12 fuel compositions
results in a lower \keff value than the pure fuel at each burnup step. 
The \gls{EBR} and Y-12 fuel compositions result in \keff values 
774-1220 and 778-1339 pcm lower than using the pure fuel, respectively. 
The largest difference is at the beginning of life, and 
the smallest difference is at the end of life. The difference between 
the \keff from downblended stockpiles and the pure fuel 
fuel is outside the error on the \keff values, which means that that 
the impurities present in the fuel decrease the neutron multiplication 
in the core. However, the \keff remains supercritical throughout the 
entire operation, and it unlikely to prevent expected operation and lifetime 
of the reactor.

\subsubsection{Neutron flux}
The thermal radial neutron flux (Figure \ref{fig:mmr_thermal_radial})
has local maximums in the middle of each of the graphite blocks and 
a global maximum in the middle of the core. These features match expectations 
as graphite is the moderator in this reactor system, and therefore will 
have a larger population of thermal neutrons. There are local minimums 
near the fuel channels in the reactor, which match expectations of 
neutrons being born at at fast energy from fission events. 

The \gls{EBR} and Y-12 fuel 
result in fluxes that differ by about an order of magnitude from the 
flux resulting from the pure fuel. The error in the each of the fluxes is on 
the order of 10$^13$ n/s/cm$^2$, which is two orders of magnitude 
different from the fluxes. Therefore, the impure fuels do result in a 
different flux that can affect reactor performance. The largest differences 
occur in the middle of the graphite control blocks, which do not contain 
any fuel. These differences are not symmetrical across the core, and 
are more pronounced on the These area of the reactor are of less importance, because they 
do not contain fuel. The neutron flux from each of the fuel compositions 
through fuel-containing areas of the reactor are closer in magnitude. 

The impacts of the differences include how often the graphite blocks 
may need replacement, which is of great consequence for this reactor design 
because it is meant to operate for 20 years without refueling. Using the 
\gls{EBR} fuel results in a slightly higher flux than using the pure fuel,
which suggests that the graphite will receive more neutron damage during 
operation. Using the Y-12 fuel results in a slightly lower flux in some 
of the graphite blocks, suggesting that the blocks will receive less 
damage. Because the neutron flux is most similar near fuel channels, 
the neutron population from fissions is mostly unaffected by the difference 
in fuel composition.

The fast radial neutron flux (Figure \ref{fig:mmr_fast_radial}) shows 
a different trend across the core from the thermal flux. The flux 
has a local minimum in the control block regions and local maximums 
near fuel channels. This is consistent with neutrons born from fission 
being in the fast energy range and then thermalizing as they travel through
graphite. 

The difference in the fluxes from each fuel composition is about a 
magnitude of order smaller than the values of the flux. The 
smallest differences are at the edges of the core, which is of 
little consequence to reactor operations. The largest differences between 
the fluxes from downblended fuel and the pure fuel are in the graphite 
blocks, which is consistent with the thermal radial flux. 

Since the differences in the fluxes are primarily 
localized to the graphite block regions, this suggests spectral differences 
between the fluxes that are not captured by examining a 2-group energy 
structure. Additionally, the center of each graphite block contains 
a coolant channel, which is filled with helium in the model used. 
The low density of helium, compared with the \gls{TRISO} fuel compacts or
the graphite, may introduce some of the difference between the 
fluxes because there are fewer interactions in this region of the core.

\subsubsection{Beta-eff}
Serpent only provides one \betaEff value, no matter how many burnup steps 
are simulated. It is unclear which burnup step the \betaEff value corresponds 
to, as the \betaEff of a reactor will not remain constant during operation as 
the $^{235}$U is depleted and plutonium is produced and fissioned. 

The \betaEff value for each fuel composition, given in Table \ref{tab:mmr_betaEff},
despite all fuels having the same $^{235}$U atom fraction. The impure
fuels result in a slightly higher \betaEff than the pure fuel. However, 
the pure fuel results in a lower \betaEff than one would expect for a thermal, 
uranium-fueled reactor, which suggests that there really shouldn't be a 
difference in the \betaEff values from these fuels. 

If the increase in \betaEff is real though, then this indicates that 
a greater fraction of the neutron population are delayed. Therefore, 
there should be a higher thermal flux when using impure fuel. Additionally, 
this result means that control mechanisms used by operators (e.g., 
control rods and burnable absorbers) will be more effective.

feedback coefficients (Table \ref{tab:mmr_moderator_feedback})

fuel composition does not have a significant effect on the values. 
The lack of variation in this parameter suggests that the fuel composition 
does not affect the neutron energy spectrum near a resonance of 
graphite.

Finally, the total temperature reactivity feedback coefficients 
(Table \ref{tab:mmr_total_feedback}) are all negative for each 
fuel composition, and become more negative with increasing burnup. 
Each of the values are within error of each other and therefore 
we have confidence that the impurities present in the downblended 
fuel will not incur a positive total temperature reactivity feedback 
coefficient. The small error relative to the coefficient values 
suggests that the trend line is quite linear, which suggests that there 
is no spectral effect from changing the material temperatures (i.e. that 
the temperatures are not near any resonances in the material cross sections.)
